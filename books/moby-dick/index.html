Moby-Dick

by Herman Melville

40 chapters

Chapter 1

Ishmael introduces himself and explains his compulsion to go to sea whenever life on land turns grim. He muses on humanity's universal attraction to water — crowds gather at the edges of rivers, oceans, and harbors as if magnetically drawn. He decides to sign on for a whaling voyage, pulled by curiosity about the great whale itself. The Fates, he jokes, have cast him in a minor role between grander events in history.

Chapter 2

Ishmael arrives in New Bedford on a bitter December Saturday night. The packet to Nantucket has already sailed; he must wait until Monday. With little money and knowing no one, he wanders the dark, freezing streets looking for cheap lodging. He passes two expensive inns and stumbles into a Black church before finding the dim, shabby Spouter-Inn, kept by a man named Peter Coffin.

Chapter 3

Ishmael explores the inn: a mysterious smoke-blackened painting of a whale impaling itself on a sinking ship's masts, walls hung with harpoons and cannibals' clubs. The inn is full — he must share a bed with an unknown harpooneer. He waits anxiously. When the harpooneer finally arrives, he is Queequeg: heavily tattooed, purple-complexioned, carrying an embalmed head and a tomahawk-pipe, performing idol-worship by candlelight. After initial terror, Ishmael reasons that "a sober cannibal is better than a drunken Christian" and they sleep soundly together.

Chapter 4

Ishmael wakes to find Queequeg's tattooed arm thrown over him. The patchwork quilt and the arm blend together in colour. He recalls a childhood memory of waking in the dark to feel a supernatural hand holding his — an experience that paralyzed him with nameless dread. He watches Queequeg's comic morning toilet: boots put on under the bed, face shaved with a harpoon blade. Queequeg departs proudly, harpoon carried like a marshal's baton.

Chapter 5

The bar-room fills with whalemen of every description. At breakfast they sit in bashful silence — brave men who have faced whales on the open sea, now sheepish at a social table. The exception is Queequeg, who sits at the head, entirely at ease, using his harpoon to spear beefsteaks and pull them toward him.

Chapter 6

Ishmael strolls the streets of New Bedford and marvels at the exotic crowd: cannibals, Fijians, Malays, and green country boys come to seek their fortune in the whale-fishery. He reflects on the astonishing wealth whaling has brought to the town — grand mansions, opulent gardens, all "harpooned and dragged up from the bottom of the sea."

Chapter 7

Ishmael visits the Whalemen's Chapel. The congregation sits in muffled silence before memorial tablets listing sailors drowned, towed out of sight by whales, or killed in the fishery. He meditates on the particular grief of those whose dead have no graves, and on the meaning of death. He concludes, defiantly, that the soul cannot be destroyed: "stave my soul, Jove himself cannot."

Chapter 8

Father Mapple arrives at the chapel — a vigorous old man who was once a sailor and harpooneer. He dramatically climbs the ship-prow-shaped pulpit by a rope ladder, then hauls the ladder up behind him, sealing himself off from the world. Ishmael reads this as a symbol of spiritual self-isolation required for true preaching.

Chapter 9

Father Mapple delivers a thunderous sermon on Jonah: his disobedience to God, his guilty flight aboard a ship to Tarshish, the storm, the casting of lots, the whale. He draws two lessons — for sinners, true repentance is not demanding pardon but accepting punishment; for preachers, the duty is to speak truth even to a hostile world. The sermon ends with an ecstatic declaration that the greatest delight belongs to the man who acknowledges no lord but God.

Chapter 10

Back at the Spouter-Inn, Ishmael watches Queequeg solemnly counting the pages of a large book in groups of fifty, astonished by the total. Drawn to Queequeg's quiet dignity and self-possession, Ishmael approaches. They share a pipe, bond immediately, and Queequeg declares them "married" — bosom friends in his culture's phrase. He divides his silver coins equally and gives Ishmael half. That evening they join together in Queequeg's idol-worship, Ishmael reasoning that to worship with a friend is simply to do the will of God.

Chapter 11

Ishmael and Queequeg lie awake in the dark, chatting contentedly. Ishmael meditates on the pleasure of warmth-by-contrast: you can only truly feel warm when some small part of you is cold. Queequeg smokes his tomahawk-pipe between them. He begins, in his broken English, to tell Ishmael his history.

Chapter 12

Queequeg is a prince from the remote island of Rokovoko, son of a High Chief. Burning to see Christendom, he secretly stowed away on a Sag Harbour whaling ship by paddling out in a canoe and clinging to the anchor chains. Onboard, he learned whalemen's skills. But witnessing Christian behaviour in port — the drinking, the waste, the cruelty — disillusioned him completely: "it's a wicked world in all meridians; I'll die a pagan." He now agrees to ship with Ishmael to Nantucket.

Chapter 13

Ishmael and Queequeg take the schooner Moss to Nantucket. Queequeg exchanges wheelbarrow stories with Ishmael (each culture baffled by the other's customs). On board, a country bumpkin mocks Queequeg; Queequeg hurls him into the air. Minutes later, when a snapped boom sends the same man overboard, Queequeg dives in and rescues him without fanfare — "We cannibals must help these Christians." All hands vote him a noble trump.

Chapter 14

A lyrical tribute to the island: a mere sandbar, treeless, almost waterless, yet home to men who conquered the world's oceans. The Nantucketers are framed as latter-day Alexanders who have parcelled out the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian Oceans among themselves. The sea is theirs; they alone truly live on it.

Chapter 15

Ishmael and Queequeg arrive in Nantucket and find lodging at the Try Pots inn, run by the brisk Mrs. Hussey. The house is obsessed with fish — chowder for every meal, clam-shell paving, cod-vertebra necklace. They eat magnificent clam chowder, then cod chowder. At bedtime Mrs. Hussey confiscates Queequeg's harpoon, citing a previous boarder who killed himself with his.

Chapter 16

Queequeg's god Yojo has ordained that Ishmael alone must choose their ship. Ishmael tours the harbour and selects the Pequod: an ancient, weatherbeaten vessel decorated with sperm-whale teeth along her bulwarks and a tiller carved from a whale's jaw — "a cannibal of a craft." He meets the gruff Quaker co-owner Captain Peleg and the miserly Captain Bildad. After a comic haggling scene — Bildad proposing the 777th lay; Peleg insisting on the 300th — Ishmael signs on. He learns that the real captain, Ahab, lost his leg to a whale and is brooding ashore, not yet seen by anyone.

Chapter 17

Queequeg spends the day in religious fasting, seated motionless on his haunches in their locked room with his idol Yojo balanced on his head. Ishmael, unable to rouse him, panics and nearly breaks down the door — Mrs. Hussey fears another suicide. Queequeg is found perfectly serene. When the Ramadan ends, Ishmael lectures him at length on the irrationality of extreme religious practice. Queequeg listens politely and is entirely unimpressed.

Chapter 18

Ishmael brings Queequeg to sign on to the Pequod. Peleg and Bildad refuse to accept a cannibal. Ishmael talks his way around their objections by invoking the "great and everlasting First Congregation of this whole worshipping world." Queequeg clinches the argument by harpooning a small tar spot at great distance, skimming the iron over Bildad's hat. He is signed on for the 90th lay. Bildad presses a religious tract into his hands; Peleg warns that "pious harpooneers never make good voyagers."

Chapter 19

Leaving the Pequod, Ishmael and Queequeg are stopped by a ragged, pockmarked stranger who calls himself Elijah. He makes dark, cryptic allusions to Captain Ahab — a Cape Horn episode, a "scrimshage with a Spaniard before the altar," a silver calabash, a prophecy about the leg. Ishmael dismisses him as a humbug. But Elijah follows them at a distance, and afterward the encounter lingers uneasily in Ishmael's mind.

Chapter 20

The Pequod is being furiously provisioned for departure: sails mended, rigging overhauled, the hold stocked. Bildad's warm-hearted sister Aunt Charity bustles aboard daily with jars of pickles, rolls of flannel, and eventually an oil-ladle and a whaling-lance. Captain Ahab remains ashore, still unseen. Ishmael tries to suppress his growing unease about sailing under a captain he has never laid eyes on.

Chapter 21

In the grey pre-dawn, Ishmael and Queequeg head to the Pequod and pass Elijah one last time. He mutters darkly about shadows he thought he saw boarding the ship in the night. They find the vessel hushed and apparently empty, then discover a rigger who confirms that Ahab came aboard late the previous night. Starbuck is already active on deck. The mystery of Ahab deepens as the ship readies to sail.

Chapter 22

The Pequod departs on Christmas Day. Captain Peleg roars commands and literally kicks reluctant hands into action; Captain Bildad paces the deck singing psalms. As the ship clears the harbour and the pilots prepare to disembark, Bildad's farewell becomes unexpectedly tender — he calls out last reminders about flannel waistcoats and not forgetting to say prayers. The pilots descend into their boat and the Pequod plunges out into the wintry Atlantic alone.

Chapter 23

A short, elegiac chapter. Bulkington, the powerful sailor briefly glimpsed in New Bedford, is seen at the helm as the ship heads out into the gale. Ishmael meditates on his meaning: the soul that seeks safety in harbour will be dashed on the lee shore; only by driving into the open, howling sea can it survive. "In landlessness alone resides the highest truth." Bulkington is given a six-inch chapter as his "stoneless grave."

Chapter 24

Ishmael mounts a formal defence of whaling against those who consider it vulgar or ignoble. He argues that whalers have been the true pioneers of global exploration — they opened Australia, Antarctica, and the Pacific islands to the world. Whaling sustains entire economies and navies. The whale itself is the mightiest creature on earth, making its hunters a heroic order. "A whale-ship was my Yale College and my Harvard."

Chapter 25

A brief, sardonic addendum to the defence of whaling: sperm oil is used to anoint monarchs at their coronations, from British kings to foreign potentates. Whalers therefore supply the sacred oil that consecrates royalty. Ishmael notes this with dry satisfaction and leaves the moral to the reader.

Chapter 26

The first of two chapters introducing the Pequod's officers. Starbuck, the chief mate, is a lean, earnest Nantucket Quaker — careful, conscientious, and brave, but with a bravery rooted in deep awareness of danger rather than its absence. His courage is not the reckless kind: "I will have no man in my boat who is not afraid of a whale." Ishmael pauses to meditate on the dignity of common men, arguing that democratic ideals demand we honour the meanest mariners and renegades as fully human.

Chapter 27

The second mate, Stubb, is a Cape Cod man — easy-going, good-humoured, pipe always in his mouth, treating death as casually as a change of watch. The third mate, Flask, is a pugnacious Vineyard man who regards whales as oversized water-rats to be destroyed. Each mate commands a whaleboat crewed by one of the three harpooneers: Queequeg serves under Starbuck; Tashtego, a Gay-Head Indian of fierce bearing, under Stubb; Daggoo, a towering African of magnificent physique, under Flask. The crew at large is an international assembly of "Isolatoes" — men from every corner of the world who share only the sea. Pip, a small Black boy from Alabama who beats the tambourine, is mentioned in passing.

Chapter 28

Ahab appears on deck for the first time. He is a tall, broad man of bronzed, weathered aspect — neither old nor young, but aged beyond years. A livid white scar runs from his hairline down the full length of his body, as if he had been struck by lightning. One leg is ivory, carved from a sperm-whale's jaw, its base planted in an auger-hole drilled into the deck. He stands at the rail staring fixedly ahead with an expression of unbending, unsurrenderable will. He says nothing. As the weather warms on the voyage south, he begins to appear on deck more regularly.

Chapter 29

Warm tropical weather draws Ahab to pace the deck through the night, his ivory leg hammering a relentless rhythm that disturbs the sleeping crew. Stubb politely approaches and suggests he muffle the leg. Ahab erupts — calls him a dog and orders him below. Stubb retreats, stunned and baffled, and delivers a long soliloquy in his hammock trying to make sense of the encounter. He decides Ahab must be "a sort of a strange critter, and not a common man."

Chapter 30

A very short chapter. After Stubb leaves, Ahab lights his pipe and settles into his chair. But the smoke gives him no pleasure — his agitated mind cannot be calmed by it. He looks at the pipe for a moment, then hurls it into the sea. It is a small, telling act: he has passed beyond the reach of ordinary comfort.

Chapter 31

Stubb tells Flask about a dream: Ahab kicked him with the ivory leg, and when Stubb raged at the insult, a humpbacked merman appeared and told him to regard it as an honour — a kick from a great man is worth more than a punch from a lesser one. Stubb woke, half-convinced. Meanwhile Ahab has already ordered extra lookouts aloft, calling for them to watch specifically for a white whale. The hunt has quietly begun.

Chapter 32

Ishmael interrupts the narrative for an extended scientific classification of whales. He divides the order into three "Books" by size: the Folio (Sperm Whale, Right Whale, Fin-Back, Hump-Back, Razor-Back, Sulphur-Bottom), the Octavo (Grampus, Black Fish, Narwhale, Killer, Thrasher), and the Duodecimo (three species of porpoise). Each species receives a brief description covering its habitat, physical traits, and usefulness to the fishery. Ishmael acknowledges his system is imperfect and leaves it deliberately unfinished, "like the great Cathedral of Cologne" — a structure whose incompleteness is itself a testament to human ambition.

Chapter 33

An essay on shipboard authority and ceremony. In the old Dutch whale-fishery, the "Specksynder" — the Chief Harpooneer — was the true commander; the captain was a later, landlubber imposition. Ishmael reflects on how Ahab maintains his authority through ceremony and mystique, keeping himself partly absent and remote so that his presence, when felt, strikes with full force. True greatness, he concludes, cannot be explained by office or rank alone — it must be "plucked from the skies."

Chapter 34

A comic account of mealtimes aboard the Pequod. Ahab dines first, in heavy silence, and the three mates sit in reverential tension, barely daring to speak. Starbuck ventures a remark; Stubb tries a joke that lands badly; Flask eats in frantic haste, always last in and first out, perpetually half-starved. After the mates withdraw, the three harpooneers take their places at the table and eat with magnificent, terrifying appetite, reducing the timid steward Dough-Boy to a state of permanent nervous collapse.

Chapter 35

Ishmael describes taking his first turn at the mast-head lookout. He surveys the history of elevated watching posts — Egyptians atop the Pyramids, Napoleon on his column, Washington on his monument. He warns against the danger of sending a dreamy, philosophically-inclined young man aloft: such a person, lulled by the sea's motion and his own reverie, will lose all sense of self, merge with the infinite, and forget entirely to watch for whales — then tumble from the mast into the sea below.

Chapter 36

The pivotal scene of the novel. Ahab summons all hands to the quarter-deck and nails a sixteen-dollar Spanish gold doubloon to the main-mast: it belongs to whoever first sights Moby Dick, the White Whale. He confirms that it was Moby Dick who reaped off his leg. Starbuck challenges him — to seek vengeance on a dumb animal is madness; it is blasphemy to hate a beast. Ahab responds with his famous "pasteboard masks" speech: all visible things are but walls and illusions; the white whale is the mask through which he strikes at the inscrutable force behind the world. The harpooneers cross their weapons and drink from the iron sockets of their harpoons in a dark pagan oath. The entire crew is swept up in Ahab's frenzy.

Chapter 37

Ahab alone at the stern windows as the sun goes down. He delivers a soliloquy of iron resolve mixed with a sense of doom: he feels himself cursed, driven by some power beyond his will, yet accepts this with fierce pride. "The path to my fixed purpose is laid with iron rails, whereon my soul is grooved to run." He acknowledges he is damned and keeps going anyway.

Chapter 38

Starbuck's soliloquy at the mainmast in the gathering dark. He sees with terrible clarity that Ahab's madness will destroy them all, and he cannot stop it — his duty of obedience and Ahab's force of will overmaster his reason. "I think I see his impious end; but feel that I must help him to it." He prays that some power from outside might intervene where he cannot. His helplessness and his grief are rendered with quiet dignity.

Chapter 39

Stubb's brief soliloquy, a counterpoint to Starbuck's anguish. He resolves the whole matter with a laugh: "A laugh's the wisest, easiest answer to all that's queer." He will think of nothing, whistle, and let things fall as they will. The three soliloquies of Ahab, Starbuck, and Stubb form a triptych of responses to catastrophe — pride, grief, and resignation.

Chapter 40

A theatrical ensemble scene in near-dramatic form, with speech headings for sailors of different nationalities. In the wake of Ahab's quarter-deck ceremony, the crew celebrates below decks — singing, dancing, Pip beating his tambourine. A squall line approaches. Tensions flare: Daggoo and a Spanish sailor nearly come to blows over a racial insult. The wind rises to a shriek. In the tumult, Pip, small and terrified, crouches and prays aloud to God to protect "this one poor fin-back whale" of a Black boy from those who have "no bowels to feel fear in." The chapter ends in darkness and storm.
